Protocol lab — 01

BB84 quantum key distribution

Generate qubits, send them through a lossy/noisy fiber channel, let Bob measure in random bases, estimate QBER from a public sample, and compute the remaining secret key after privacy amplification.

🔑 What is BB84? — Expand for a first-principles explanation

The core idea

Alice encodes each bit as a photon polarization, randomly choosing between two incompatible bases: rectilinear (Z: ↑/→) or diagonal (X: ↗/↘). Bob also randomly picks a basis to measure in. When they chose the same basis, Bob's result matches Alice's — these bits are kept. When bases differ, results are random and the bit is discarded. This is called sifting.

Key insight: No physical law can copy an unknown quantum state (no-cloning theorem). Eve must guess the basis, disturbing ~25% of kept bits if she intercepts. This raises the Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER) above ~11%, signalling her presence.

What to try first

1. Run with default settings — note the QBER and final key length.
2. Slowly increase Optical misalignment and watch QBER rise.
3. Set Eve intercept-resend to 50%+ — the key should abort.
4. Run Monte Carlo to see the statistical distribution of QBER across many trials.
5. Increase fiber distance and observe how fewer detected pulses create noisier QBER estimates.

⚡ Real-world connection
The 11% QBER threshold shown here is the approximate abort point for idealized BB84. Real systems (like KPN's network) use decoy-state BB84 with finite-key security proofs that account for pulse statistics and detector attacks.

Photon-pair lab — 02

Time-bin entanglement and BBM92

Model an entangled photon-pair source, two telecom fiber arms, detector noise, Franson interference visibility, CHSH Bell violation, and the secure key rate for an entanglement-based QKD experiment.

⚛️ What is time-bin entanglement? — Click to expand

Time-bin encoding

Instead of polarization, photons are encoded in early or late arrival time slots. A pair of entangled photons is created so that neither photon alone has a definite time-bin — they share a superposition. This makes the entanglement robust against polarization rotation in fiber, which is why it's preferred for metropolitan quantum networks.

Key insight: The Franson interferometer converts time-bin phase coherence into two-photon interference fringes. The fringe visibility V directly reveals entanglement quality. A CHSH S-value above 2 proves Bell inequality violation — no classical physics can explain the correlations.

What to try first

1. Run with default settings — note the S-value and whether Bell is violated.
2. Increase Phase noise — watch the fringe wash out and S drop below 2.
3. Try Scan distance to see how far the entanglement can reach before the key rate collapses.
4. Compare C-band 1550 nm vs O-band 1310 nm — note the different attenuation rates.
5. High dark counts destroy visibility even with good source quality.

⚡ Real-world connection
The KPN Hague–Delft metropolitan fiber link uses time-bin entanglement at 1310 nm (O-band) distributed over deployed dark fiber, demonstrating coexistence with bidirectional C-band classical traffic.

Network lab — 03

Entanglement routing in a multi-node network

Create metropolitan-style topologies, choose an end-to-end route, and account for fiber loss, Bell-state measurement success, memory decoherence, and optional purification rounds.

🔗 How do quantum repeaters work? — Click to expand

Why you can't just amplify

Classical networks repeat signals by measuring and re-transmitting. Quantum networks cannot copy unknown qubits (no-cloning theorem). Instead, neighbouring nodes generate entangled pairs, store them in quantum memories, and perform Bell-state measurements (BSMs) that "swap" entanglement across the node — extending the entanglement without ever reading the qubit value.

Key insight: Each entanglement swap degrades fidelity slightly. Purification (entanglement distillation) can recover fidelity by consuming extra entangled pairs, but at the cost of throughput. Quantum memories must hold qubits long enough for heralding signals to propagate — this coherence time is critical.

What to try first

1. Run the diamond topology — observe the routing log to see which path is chosen.
2. Reduce Memory lifetime to 5 ms — watch fidelity collapse.
3. Increase Purification rounds to 3 — fidelity improves but throughput drops.
4. Try Attempt burst to see the distribution of success probabilities per attempt.
5. Switch to ring or mesh topology and see how redundant paths change routing options.

⚡ Real-world connection
The TU/e PhD project specifically targets routing protocols for multi-node quantum networks integrated into KPN's metropolitan fiber infrastructure, including how to schedule swaps and memory usage across multiple simultaneous users.

Telecom coexistence lab — 04

Quantum signals beside classical WDM traffic

Explore how wavelength choice, launch power, WDM channel count, filter bandwidth, isolation, and fiber length affect Raman noise, QBER, and secret key rate on a shared fiber.

📡 Why does coexistence matter? — Click to expand

The Raman scattering problem

Telecom operators want to use existing deployed fiber for quantum signals alongside classical data. But classical light (even in different WDM channels) causes spontaneous Raman scattering — photons generated at all wavelengths, including the quantum channel's wavelength. These Raman photons create noise clicks that raise QBER and can kill the secret key rate completely.

Key insight: Using the O-band (1310 nm) for the quantum channel while keeping classical traffic in the C-band (1530–1565 nm) provides ~240 nm of spectral separation, dramatically reducing Raman overlap. This is the strategy used in real KPN deployments.

What to try first

1. Compare O-band 1310 nm vs C-band 1550 nm quantum wavelengths — observe the Raman noise difference.
2. Increase Classical power/channel from −10 to +10 dBm and watch QBER degrade.
3. Increase WDM channels to 40 (full C-band) — see the noise multiply.
4. Use Scan power to find the maximum safe classical launch power.
5. Adjust Classical isolation — high isolation filters are the primary engineering mitigation.

⚡ Real-world connection
The TU/e PhD project explicitly lists coexistence of classical telecom traffic and quantum signals as a core research topic, including noise, crosstalk, scalability, and interoperability in telecom-grade quantum networks.

Quick reference — 05

Glossary of quantum networking

Key terms, physical meanings, and the equations that govern them. Use this while running simulations to connect controls to their underlying physics.

QBER — Quantum Bit Error Rate
The fraction of sifted key bits where Bob's measurement disagrees with Alice's intended bit. Caused by optical misalignment, dark counts, and eavesdropping. The BB84 abort threshold is approximately 11% for one-way post-processing.
QBER = errors / sifted_bits_tested
Sifting
After quantum transmission, Alice and Bob publicly announce which basis (Z or X) each used. Bits where they chose different bases are discarded because they carry no correlated information. Roughly 50% of pulses survive sifting in BB84.
Privacy Amplification
A classical hashing step that compresses the sifted key, removing any information that Eve might have learned. The surviving fraction is approximately max(0, 1 − 2h₂(QBER)) where h₂ is the binary entropy function.
f_secret ≈ max(0, 1 − 2·h₂(QBER))
h₂(p) = −p·log₂p − (1−p)·log₂(1−p)
No-cloning Theorem
It is impossible to create an identical copy of an unknown quantum state. This is why quantum networks cannot amplify signals classically, and why eavesdropping always disturbs the quantum channel.
Time-bin Entanglement
Entanglement encoded in the arrival time of photons (early vs late time slots). Robust against polarization drift in fiber, making it preferred for metropolitan quantum networks and deployed fiber experiments.
Franson Interferometer
An unbalanced Mach-Zehnder interferometer used to measure time-bin entanglement. Two-photon coincidences vary sinusoidally with phase: C(φ) = C₀[1+V·cos(φ)]/2. The visibility V directly quantifies entanglement quality.
C(φ) = C₀[1 + V·cos(φ)] / 2
CHSH Inequality & Bell Test
A statistical test of quantum non-locality. The CHSH parameter S = 2√2·V for ideal time-bin sources. Classical physics requires S ≤ 2. A measured S > 2 proves genuine quantum entanglement. Maximum quantum value is 2√2 ≈ 2.828.
S = 2√2 · V
Bell violation if S > 2
Tsirelson bound: S ≤ 2√2
Entanglement Fidelity
Measures how close a distributed state is to a perfect Bell state. A fidelity of 1 is ideal; fidelity ≤ 0.5 means the state is not entangled at all. After multiple swaps and memory decoherence, fidelity degrades along the route.
F = (1 + V) / 2
F = 1 ideal, F ≤ 0.5 no entanglement
Entanglement Swapping
A Bell-state measurement (BSM) at an intermediate node "teleports" entanglement to the outer nodes, extending it without measuring the qubit value. Each swap probabilistically succeeds and reduces fidelity slightly.
F_swap ≈ F₁·F₂ + (1−F₁)·(1−F₂)/3
(simplified Werner-state model)
Quantum Memory & Decoherence
Quantum memories store entangled qubits while heralding signals travel through the network. Qubits decay toward a mixed state exponentially with time constant τ. Long coherence times are a primary hardware challenge.
F(t) = 1/4 + (F₀ − 1/4)·exp(−t/τ)
Entanglement Purification
A protocol that takes multiple lower-fidelity entangled pairs and produces fewer higher-fidelity pairs by local operations and classical communication (LOCC). Improves quality at the cost of throughput.
Spontaneous Raman Scattering
When strong classical light travels through silica fiber, it generates photons at shifted wavelengths via inelastic scattering. These appear as noise to the quantum detector. Raman noise scales with classical power and fiber length.
N_Raman ∝ P_classical · L_fiber · Δλ_filter
WDM — Wavelength Division Multiplexing
Multiple classical data channels transmitted simultaneously at different wavelengths on the same fiber. C-band (1530–1565 nm) hosts most WDM traffic. O-band (1260–1360 nm) is used for quantum channels to reduce Raman overlap with C-band.
Dark Counts
Spurious clicks from a single-photon detector in the absence of signal photons, caused by thermal electrons or afterpulsing. Dark counts create random bits in sifted keys and accidental coincidences in entanglement measurements.
BBM92 Protocol
An entanglement-based QKD protocol (Bennett, Brassard, Mermin 1992). Alice and Bob each receive one photon from an entangled pair, measure in random bases, and extract a secret key — equivalent to BB84 but using entanglement as the resource.
R_secret ≈ 0.5 · R_coinc · max(0, 1−2·h₂(QBER))
Secret Key Rate
The net rate at which secret, secure bits are produced, after all error correction and privacy amplification. Drops exponentially with distance due to fiber loss. The practical goal of any deployed QKD system.
R = S_signal · max(0, 1 − 2·h₂(QBER)) / 2
Heralded Entanglement Generation
Entanglement between two nodes is confirmed by a classical signal (the "herald") indicating success. The generation probability per attempt drops with distance due to fiber loss and BSM success probability.
P_link ≈ η² · P_BSM
η = 10^(−α·L/10)

BB84 model — equations and assumptions

Alice randomly chooses bits and bases Z/X. Bob randomly chooses measurement bases. Bits are kept only when Alice and Bob used the same basis and Bob registered a detection. Eve is modelled as an intercept-resend attack.

Channel transmission: T = 10^(−α·L / 10) · η_detector Click probability per pulse: P_click = T + P_dark − T·P_dark Sifting: keep bit i if Bob clicked AND basis_A[i] = basis_B[i] Sampled QBER: Q = sample_errors / sample_size Secret fraction (asymptotic teaching estimate): f = max(0, 1 − 2·h₂(Q))
Model limits
The 11% abort threshold is the approximate one-way post-processing limit for idealised BB84. Real systems need decoy-state analysis, finite-key bounds, detector characterisation, and authentication.
Time-bin entanglement model

Time-bin entanglement uses early/late photon arrival modes. A Franson interferometer converts phase coherence into two-photon interference fringes. Strong visibility supports Bell violation and entanglement-based QKD such as BBM92.

Arm transmission: T_arm = 10^(−α·L/2 / 10) · 0.78 Signal coincidences: C_signal = R_pairs · T_arm² Accidental coincidences: C_acc = (R_singles)² · τ_window Franson fringe: C(φ) = C₀·[1 + V·cos(φ)] / 2 Effective visibility: V = V_intrinsic · exp(−σ²φ/2) · V_multipair · V_noise CHSH S-value: S = 2√2·V (Bell violated if S > 2)
Routing and swapping model

Quantum repeaters cannot copy qubits. Instead, neighbouring links first generate entanglement. Bell-state measurements at intermediate nodes perform entanglement swapping, extending entanglement across the route.

Route cost metric: cost(edge) = −log(P_success · F_link) Swap fidelity update (Werner-state): F_swap ≈ F₁·F₂ + (1−F₁)·(1−F₂)/3 Memory decay: F(t) = 1/4 + (F₀ − 1/4)·exp(−t/τ) Route success probability: P_route = ∏ P_link × ∏ P_BSM Throughput: R ≈ R_attempt · P_route

Purification improves fidelity but consumes additional entangled pairs, so the simulator lowers throughput when purification is enabled.

Coexistence model — Raman noise budget

Classical C-band WDM channels share fiber with quantum channels. Spontaneous Raman scattering and direct leakage create detector noise counts that increase QBER. O-band quantum channels reduce Raman overlap with C-band traffic, but O-band has higher fiber attenuation than C-band.

Raman noise rate: N_Raman ∝ P_classical · N_WDM · L · Δλ_filter / isolation Total noise: N_total = N_Raman + N_leakage + N_dark QBER: Q = Q_optical + 0.5 · N_total / (S_signal + N_total) Key rate: R_secret = 0.5 · S_signal · max(0, 1 − 2·h₂(Q))
Model limits and reproducibility

This lab is rigorous as an educational simulator: formulas are explicit, assumptions are logged, controls are reproducible, and every parameter has a documented physical meaning.

Not included in this simulator: - Decoy-state analysis for weak coherent pulse BB84 - Finite-key security confidence intervals - Detector side-channel attacks - Full polarization drift control loops - Calibrated Raman spectra from measured fiber data - Asynchronous event queues for heralding scheduling - Device-independent security proofs

The seed control in BB84 makes runs fully reproducible. The JSON export stores numerical parameters for all labs.

Time-bin entanglement and BBM92: source, CHSH, key rate

A Franson interferometer measures two-photon interference; the visibility of the sinusoidal fringe is a direct indicator of phase coherence.

Entanglement routing: topology, swapping, memories, purification

A multi-node quantum network does not route unknown qubits by copying them. It establishes entangled links between neighbouring nodes, performs Bell-state measurements at intermediate nodes (entanglement swapping), and uses classical heralding signals to confirm successes.

Fiber coexistence: WDM traffic, Raman noise, filtering, QBER

Classical C-band WDM channels can share fiber with quantum channels, but spontaneous Raman scattering and direct leakage create detector noise counts that increase QBER. O-band quantum channels often reduce Raman overlap with C-band classical traffic, but O-band has higher fiber attenuation (0.35 dB/km vs 0.20 dB/km at C-band).

Key references and further reading

These are the foundational papers and deployments that the simulator is designed to help you understand.

Protocol

Bennett & Brassard (1984)

BB84 — the original quantum key distribution protocol. Defines prepare-and-measure QKD using non-orthogonal quantum states.

Protocol

Bennett, Brassard & Mermin (1992)

BBM92 — entanglement-based extension of BB84. Alice and Bob each measure one photon from an entangled pair.

Theory

Franson (1989)

Proposed the time-bin entanglement scheme and the unbalanced Mach-Zehnder (Franson) interferometer for testing Bell inequalities with photon pairs.

Theory

Briegel et al. (1998)

Quantum repeaters — the foundational paper for entanglement swapping and purification as a means of extending quantum communication distance.

Experiment

Wengerowsky et al. (2020)

Entanglement distribution over metropolitan fiber in real deployed networks (Vienna), demonstrating O-band time-bin entanglement in urban infrastructure.

Experiment

KPN / TU Delft (2022)

First deployed metropolitan quantum network (Delft–Hague–Rotterdam). Uses time-bin entanglement at 1310 nm coexisting with classical C-band WDM traffic on KPN's fiber.
